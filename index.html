01
Infrastructure · Aviation · Germany
Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER)
2006–2020 · €7.3B · 9-year delay · Project Management Failure
DELAYED OVER BUDGET COMPLETED
Background & Scope
Berlin's new international airport was planned to open in 2011 but finally opened in 2020 — nearly 9 years late. Originally budgeted at €2.8B, final cost reached €7.3B. The airport was designed to consolidate three Berlin airports into one world-class hub serving 30M+ passengers annually.

The project involved complex systems integration: fire safety, terminal design, baggage handling, and rail connections — all managed under fragmented contracts with poor integration management.
Root Cause Analysis
1. Fire Safety System Failure: The fire suppression and smoke extraction system did not meet safety codes, requiring complete redesign.

2. Poor Governance: Politicians sat on the supervisory board without PM expertise. No experienced Program Director was appointed.

3. Design Changes During Construction: Over 65,000 design change orders were issued mid-construction.

4. Contractor Fragmentation: 60+ subcontractors with no integrated management system or single point of accountability.

02
Rail Infrastructure · UK
Crossrail — Elizabeth Line
2009–2022 · £18.9B · Complex urban mega-project
COMPLETED OVERRUN BIM DELIVERED
Background & Scope
Crossrail is a 118km east-west rail line through London, including 42km of new tunnels under the city. With 41 stations, 10 new or rebuilt, it is Europe's largest infrastructure project. The project involved TBM tunneling, complex station fit-out, rail systems integration, and operations under a live city.

Cross-functional teams from 1,000+ companies. Peak workforce of 10,000 workers. Managed through a single delivery authority — Crossrail Ltd — under NEC3 contract framework.
PM Challenges & Solutions
1. Systems Integration Delay (2018): Signaling, rolling stock, and fit-out integration caused a 3-year delay. Solution: Created an independent systems integration testing program.

2. Supply Chain Complexity: 1,000+ contracts managed through NEC3 with clear early warning systems and pain/gain sharing.

3. Safety in Live City: 26M+ working hours with zero fatal accidents. HSE Plan updated daily. Behavior-based safety program deployed.

4. BIM Level 2: Full BIM implementation across all assets, enabling digital handover.

03
Iconic Architecture · Australia
Sydney Opera House
1959–1973 · AUD$102M · The most famous PM failure turned icon
OVER BUDGET 1,457% 6-YEAR DELAY UNESCO HERITAGE
Background & Scope
Commissioned in 1957, the Sydney Opera House was originally budgeted at AUD$7M with a 1963 completion date. It finally opened in 1973 at a cost of AUD$102M — a 1,457% cost overrun — and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The project is the most famous example of the planning fallacy in construction history. It proceeded to construction before the design was complete, leading to constant rework, structural challenges with the iconic shell roof, and political controversy that led to the architect Jørn Utzon resigning in 1966.
PM Failures & Analysis
1. Construction Before Design Completion: Political pressure forced construction to begin before structural solutions for the roof shells were finalized. This led to massive rework.

2. Optimism Bias: The 7M estimate ignored complexity and unknowns — a classic planning fallacy.

3. Stakeholder Conflict: The NSW government's interference led to the architect's resignation, causing design continuity loss.

4. No Change Control: Design changes were made without formal impact assessment on cost or schedule.

04
Urban Transit · UAE
Dubai Metro — Red & Green Lines
2005–2011 · $7.6B · Delivered ON TIME for Expo 2020 legacy
ON TIME WITHIN BUDGET RECORD SPEED
Background & Scope
The Dubai Metro Red Line — 52.1km with 29 stations — was built in just under 4 years in one of the world's fastest-growing cities. It was the world's longest fully automated metro line at opening in 2009. The project was delivered by a consortium led by Mitsubishi-Obayashi under an EPC contract worth $7.6B.

Construction ran 24/7 in extreme desert heat conditions (50°C+). The project involved viaduct sections, underground tunnels, depots, and a fully integrated SCADA control system with driverless operation.
Success Factors
1. Strong Political Will: RTA (Roads & Transport Authority) acted as a decisive client with a clear mandate and unified decision chain.

2. EPC Structure: Single EPC contractor responsible for design, procurement, and construction — eliminating interface management risk.

3. 24/7 Construction: Three shifts with aggressive scheduling, supported by a massive workforce of 30,000+ at peak.

4. Early Planning: All land acquisition, utility diversions, and design were completed before major works began — rare discipline in mega-projects.

05
Highway Infrastructure · USA
Boston Big Dig — Central Artery/Tunnel
1982–2007 · $24.3B · Largest highway project in US history
190% OVERRUN 8-YEAR DELAY URBAN SUCCESS
Background & Scope
The Big Dig relocated Interstate 93 underground through the heart of downtown Boston, replacing a deteriorating elevated highway. The project included a 3.5-mile tunnel, the Ted Williams Tunnel, the Leverett Circle Connector Bridge, and the iconic Leonard P. Zakim Bridge.

Originally estimated at $2.6B, the final cost reached $24.3B, making it the most expensive highway project in US history. Construction occurred 24/7 in a dense urban environment while keeping existing highways operational — an unprecedented engineering challenge.
Why It Overran — PM Analysis
1. Scope Changes: Major design changes were made after approvals. The tunnel depth was increased, adding billions in cost.

2. Political & Community Pressure: Mitigation measures for noise, vibration, and disruption added significant unplanned costs.

3. Contractor Fraud: Concrete supplier provided substandard materials — a quality control failure with fatal consequences (ceiling collapse, 2006).

4. Utility Conflicts: 30,000+ utilities had to be relocated — discovered during construction, not pre-planned.

06
Supertall Tower · Japan
Tokyo Skytree
2008–2012 · ¥65B · World's tallest tower — on time, on budget
ON TIME ON BUDGET ZERO ACCIDENTS
Background & Scope
At 634 meters, Tokyo Skytree is the world's tallest tower and second tallest structure. Built in a dense urban environment above active rail lines in Oshiage, Tokyo. Completed in 4 years, on budget at ¥65B (~$650M).

The tower features a unique tripod base transitioning to a cylindrical upper section — designed to survive major earthquakes. A central concrete shaft with viscous dampers provides seismic isolation. The project was managed under Japanese Genba (field-first) philosophy with extreme precision and lean construction principles.
PM Success Factors
1. Kaizen Culture: Continuous improvement embedded in daily site operations. Daily stand-up meetings at every level of the workforce.

2. Modular Prefabrication: Steel components were fabricated off-site to exact tolerances, minimizing on-site time and error.

3. Seismic Engineering Integration: Structural engineering and construction planning were jointly managed — no design/build separation.

4. Zero-Accident Target: HSE was a non-negotiable KPI. Every near-miss was reported and addressed within 24 hours.

05 — Glossary

PM Terminology

EVM
Earned Value Management
Integrates scope, schedule, and cost to measure project performance and forecast completion.
WBS
Work Breakdown Structure
Hierarchical decomposition of project scope into deliverables and work packages.
CPM
Critical Path Method
Identifies the longest sequence of dependent tasks to determine minimum project duration.
RFI
Request for Information
Formal document raised by contractor to resolve design ambiguities or missing information.
NCR
Non-Conformance Report
Records work or materials that do not meet specification, triggering corrective action.
ITP
Inspection & Test Plan
Documents what inspections and tests are required, by whom, and at which hold/witness points.
RACI
Responsibility Matrix
Defines who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for each project activity.
KPI
Key Performance Indicator
Measurable metric tracking project performance against objectives: cost, schedule, quality, safety.
EPC
Engineering Procurement Construction
Single contract where one entity is responsible for complete design, procurement, and construction.
BIM
Building Information Modeling
Digital representation of a built asset integrating 3D geometry, data, and project lifecycle information.
SPI
Schedule Performance Index
EV/PV ratio measuring schedule efficiency. Below 1.0 indicates schedule delay.
CPI
Cost Performance Index
EV/AC ratio measuring cost efficiency. Below 1.0 indicates cost overrun in progress.
